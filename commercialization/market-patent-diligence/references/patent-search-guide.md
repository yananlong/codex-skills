# Patent Search Guide

Use this guide for patent landscapes, prior-art signals, assignee maps, and commercialization diligence. This is not legal advice.

## Search construction

1. Extract the technical claim in neutral language, not just the paper's branding.
2. Build synonym clusters:
   - mechanism or material terms
   - function or performance terms
   - application/workflow terms
   - industry vocabulary
   - old and new names for the same concept
   - abbreviations, chemical names, model names, device names, and standards terms
3. Add assignee/applicant and inventor terms from the research asset, institution, likely licensees, and competitors.
4. Search classes when keyword results are noisy: CPC, IPC, USPC where relevant, MeSH for biomedical terms, and product/industry codes for market mapping.
5. Search by problem and use case, not only by solution. Competitors may patent a different mechanism for the same workflow pain.

## Recommended source sequence

1. Start broad with patent-office or high-coverage discovery tools: USPTO, WIPO PATENTSCOPE, EPO Espacenet, and optionally Google Patents/Lens for leads.
2. Confirm important records in official office/register sources.
3. Check family members and priority dates to avoid double-counting.
4. Check assignment and applicant history when ownership matters.
5. Review citations and examiner-cited prior art for adjacent blocking or substitute technologies.
6. Check legal-status/register data only as a signal; record the source and jurisdiction.

## Patent record fields

| Field | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| publication_or_patent_number | Include country/kind code when available. |
| title | Copy or paraphrase accurately. |
| applicant_or_assignee | Record raw name and normalized name separately when needed. |
| inventors | Include only when relevant for institution/startup linkage. |
| priority_date | Use for timing and prior-art ordering. |
| publication_or_grant_date | Distinguish application publication from granted patent. |
| jurisdiction | US, WO/PCT, EP, CN, JP, KR, etc. |
| family_id_or_family_members | Avoid overcounting family members. |
| classes | CPC/IPC or other classification. |
| claim_theme | Short, source-grounded description of what appears to be claimed. |
| relevance | high / medium / low, tied to the commercialization thesis. |
| citations | Forward/backward citations when material. |
| assignment_or_legal_status_signal | Source and date required; avoid legal conclusions. |
| limitations | Missing jurisdictions, uncertain assignee, machine translation, stale status, etc. |

## Landscape interpretation

- Treat dense patenting by incumbents as a signal for competitive attention and possible blocking complexity, not automatic market attractiveness.
- Treat sparse patenting as ambiguous: it may mean white space, weak demand, trade-secret strategy, or poor search coverage.
- Watch for university-owned patents with broad claims but no productization path.
- Watch for claims that cover a component while the commercial wedge requires a full workflow solution.
- Distinguish blocking claims, background patents, design-around patents, implementation patents, and non-practicing research publications.

## Legal caveats

Do not conclude infringement, validity, enforceability, patentability, or freedom to operate. Say "signals to review with counsel" when legal meaning matters. For commercialization decisions, translate patent findings into business questions: licensing targets, crowded areas, design-around hypotheses, diligence priorities, and partner risks.
